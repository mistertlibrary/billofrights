Rights on the Docket: The Bill of Rights Today

From 1791 to this morning's news, you'll research a rights issue and identify the people with the power to decide it.

1

Choose an Issue

Start with the amendment you researched and today's news. Look for a disagreement real people are having right now.

  1. Where does your amendment show up in daily life at school, in your community, or online?
  2. What did your amendment intend to protect in 1791? What do people argue it protects today?
  3. Who would gain or lose if your issue were decided the other way?
  4. Can you name a recent news story that made you ask, "Is that allowed?"

Some Amendments Are Quieter

Not every amendment makes headlines! The Supreme Court has never decided a case on the basis of the Third Amendment. If your amendment is quiet, look for the larger idea it protects, such as the privacy of the home, and follow that idea into the news.

Scan the News

  • research.ebsco.comNewspaper Source Plus

    Newspaper Source Plus: get a load of all these newspapers!

  • research.ebsco.comTOPICsearch

    Another flavor of EBSCO; I'd suggest Explora unless you're specifically after information on recent events.

  • nytimes.comThe New York Times

    Whatever one makes of its opinion writers, the Times is the first port of call for many news-seekers for a reason. Home to some of the finest journalists in the world and long considered the standard-bearer of the American free press.

Cite as you go

Add each article to NoodleTools the day you find it. Record the author, the newspaper, and the date of publication.

2

Read Your Amendment

Read the words of your amendment, then read how scholars and judges have argued about them since 1791.

The Text and Its Meaning

  • National Constitution CenterInteractive Constitution

    Two scholars with different views write about each clause. They explain together what they agree on, then each argues the points where they part ways. The scholars are chosen with help from the American Constitution Society and the Federalist Society.

  • Library of CongressConstitution Annotated

    Congress's official record of what the Constitution means, written by the Congressional Research Service at the Library of Congress. Each clause comes with its history and the Supreme Court cases that shaped it.

  • ABC-CLIOAmerican Government

    This is a specialized database that supports guided inquiry: you ask a question that the database materials help you answer. ABC-Clio's academic success corner is integrated into all of their databases and offers fantastic tutorials on core skills like research, writing, reading, and critical thinking that I highly recommend.

Who Does the Bill of Rights Bind?

In Barron v. Baltimore (1833), the Supreme Court held that the Bill of Rights limited only the federal government. Today the Court applies most of it to state and local governments through the Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause, a doctrine called incorporation. That's why school board cases can make it to the Supreme Court.

Where It Came From

  • National ArchivesThe Bill of Rights: A Transcription

    Congress proposed twelve amendments in 1789; the states ratified ten of them on December 15, 1791. Read a transcription of the resolution on display in the National Archives Rotunda. (Potentially interesting: which amendments were dropped? One is still pending; the other became the 27th Amendment in 1992!)

  • New Jersey State ArchivesNew Jersey’s Bill of Rights

    On November 20, 1789, New Jersey became the first state to ratify the Bill of Rights. See images and a transcription of the state's own parchment copy.

  • GaleGale in Context: US History

    Gale's sheer scope and integrated features set it apart from other vendors. Its topic finder helps you make connections between terms and topics that you might not otherwise think of; its highlighting and note-taking tools are helpful if you prefer digital annotation to analog; its depth of content surfaces a wide array of primary and secondary materials on most topics.

Cite as you go

Most databases offer a ready-made citation. Treat it as a draft: bring it into NoodleTools and check each element against the source itself.

3

Find Perspectives

Find at least two people or groups who see your issue differently. Learn what each wants and why.

Start With the Databases

  • research.ebsco.comPoints of View Ultimate

    The database covers hundreds of debate topics, each with Overview (objective background/description), Point (argument) and Counterpoint (opposing argument) articles and a Guide to Critical Analysis to help the reader evaluate the controversy, read more critically, develop their own perspective, and write or debate an effective argument.

  • InfobaseIssues & Controversies

    I'd contend that Infobase's complicated issues database offers the best discovery tools. Make a point of using the subject clusters and browsing options. Their source list is a clever way to identify reliable outlets for all sorts of projects (not only those related to complicated issues!).

  • GaleGale in Context: Opposing Viewpoints

    I'd consider Gale the most capable of the complicated issue databases. Its topic finder helps you make connections between terms and topics that you might not otherwise think of; its highlighting and note-taking tools are helpful if you prefer digital annotation to analog; its depth of content surfaces more competent prose than many written-to-order topic overviews. Use it when style and substance are equally critical considerations.

Perspective or Source?

A source is where you found information. A perspective is a view held by a person or group with a stake in the issue. One article can report several perspectives, and two articles can share one. For each perspective, ask who holds it and why.

Courts and Cases

  • OyezOyez

    Plain-English summaries of Supreme Court cases, with audio of the arguments synced to transcripts. A free project of Cornell's Legal Information Institute, Justia, and Chicago-Kent College of Law.

  • Annenberg ClassroomAnnenberg Classroom

    Free documentaries on landmark Supreme Court cases, including a film on student free speech from Tinker v. Des Moines to Mahanoy Area School District v. B.L.

Two Readings of the Constitution

  • The Heritage FoundationThe Heritage Guide to the Constitution

    A clause-by-clause guide written by more than 150 jurists, scholars, and practitioners committed to originalism, the view that the Constitution means what it meant when ratified. Read it as one perspective, not a neutral reference!

  • theusconstitution.orgConstitutional Accountability Center

    A progressive think tank and law firm that argues the Constitution, amended over generations, is a progressive document. Like Heritage, it reads the text and its history; unlike Heritage, it reaches progressive conclusions. Browse its Issues pages, and read it as one perspective, not a neutral reference!

More Perspectives

  • BritannicaProCon

    Pro and con arguments on more than 100 debatable topics, with sources for each. Check the date: topics marked archived are no longer updated.

  • Freedom ForumFreedom Forum

    Explainers and lessons on the five freedoms of the First Amendment. Some NewseumED resources require a free account.

Cite as you go

For each perspective, cite the source where you found it and note whose view it is.

4

Find Your Decision-Maker

Work out who has the power to change or preserve your issue, and how a citizen can reach them.

  1. Is this a federal, state, or local matter?
  2. Which branch handles it: the one that makes laws, carries them out, or interprets them?
  3. Who holds that role right now? Name the person or body.
  4. How can a citizen reach them or influence them?

When a Court Decides

Judges decide the cases in front of them, and you cannot lobby a judge the way you can lobby a lawmaker. If a court holds power over your issue, look for the people who answer to voters and can respond: lawmakers who write laws and agencies that write rules.

Look It Up

  • house.govFind Your Representative

    Enter a ZIP code to find your member of the U.S. House of Representatives and a link to contact them.

  • njleg.govNew Jersey Legislature

    Find your state senator and two members of the General Assembly by district, and search bills in Trenton.

  • regulations.govRegulations.gov

    Federal agencies post proposed rules here. While a comment period is open, anyone may submit a comment.
